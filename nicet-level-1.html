Recommended study order

  1. Identify components and read gauges — pipe, hangers, bracing, sprinklers, fittings, FDC, hose connections, and supply/system gauges.
  2. Locate and identify control valves, drain valves, and test valves — and confirm the indicator position of each control valve.
  3. Identify system types and water supply — wet, dry, and standpipe systems; fire pumps, tanks, and municipal supply.
  4. Identify alarm and supervisory devices — and verify trouble, supervisory, and alarm signals.
  5. Prepare preliminary documentation — select the correct report, inventory components, and record make, type, and model.
  6. Operate components under direct supervision — exercise control valves, test the water-flow alarm, perform the main drain test, and flush hydrants.

Study guides

Code-use reminder: This guide intentionally summarizes concepts rather than reproducing NFPA text. Exact intervals, acceptance criteria, exceptions, and section numbers must be checked in your licensed NFPA 25 (2023) copy.
1.1 — Safety

Be ready to explain: personal protective equipment (PPE), lockout/tagout (LOTO), common jobsite hazards, ladder and lift use, and tool condition — and how a system that is being tested can create an impairment.

  • Wear and maintain required PPE, and inspect tools and ground-fault circuit interrupters (GFCI) before use.
  • Recognize mechanical and electrical lockout/tagout, and when it applies to a water-based system task.
  • Recognize hazards such as confined spaces, floor and ceiling openings, poor lighting, ladders, and aerial or scissor lifts.
  • Recognize when work creates a safety or system impairment, and raise it to your supervising technician before proceeding.
1.2 — Identify condition and operation of system components

This is the largest Level I domain. You are expected to reliably locate and identify equipment, read gauges, and recognize damage — without necessarily operating the system.

  • Locate and read supply-side and system-side gauges, and recognize a reading that looks abnormal.
  • Locate and identify control valves, drain valves, and test valves, and confirm the indicator position of each control valve (open versus shut).
  • Visually inspect pipe, hangers, seismic bracing, sprinklers, fittings, the fire department connection (FDC), and hose connections for damage or deficiencies.
  • Identify electronic and mechanical alarm and supervisory devices, and verify trouble, supervisory, and alarm signals.
  • Identify the system type — wet, dry, or standpipe — and the water supply, including fire pumps, tanks, and municipal supply.

Level I mindset: Identification first. Know what the component is, where it belongs, and whether it looks right — then report what you find to a Level II or III technician.

1.3 — Prepare preliminary documentation

Accurate records begin at Level I. You support the inspection by capturing an accurate picture of what is installed.

  • Identify the correct report for the ITM task being performed.
  • Inventory system components and record the size, make, type, and model of sprinklers and valves.
  • Prepare preliminary reports under the direction of a Level II or III technician.
  • Note any damage, missing components, or questionable conditions so they can be evaluated.
1.4 — Operate components under direct supervision

At Level I you perform hands-on tasks while supervised. Know the purpose of each task and the safe sequence before touching the system.

  • Fully exercise control valves — OS&Y valves, post indicator valves (PIVs), wall post indicator valves (WPIVs), and butterfly valves — and return them to the correct position.
  • Test the water-flow alarm, including the water motor gong, pressure switch, and vane-type devices.
  • Perform the main drain test to confirm the water supply is available and the supply path is not obstructed.
  • Flush hydrants, exercise hose connections, and test electronic valve supervisory devices.
  • Before operating anything, coordinate the impairment and notification steps with your supervising technician.
A control valve left in the wrong position after a task can take the system out of service. Always confirm valves are returned to normal and supervisory signals are restored.
NFPA 25 (2023) at a glance — inspect, test, maintain

NFPA 25 is the Standard for the Inspection, Testing, and Maintenance of Water-Based Fire Protection Systems. The building owner is responsible for keeping the system in service; the contractor performs the ITM tasks the standard requires and reports what is found.

ActivityWhat it meansExample
InspectionA visual check that a component appears to be in good physical condition and in its normal operating state.Looking at a sealed control valve to confirm it is open and the seal is intact.
TestA physical procedure (flow, pressure, trip, signal) that proves a component performs its function.Flowing the inspector’s test connection to sound the water motor gong.
MaintenanceWork that keeps equipment operable or repairs it — preventive and corrective.Lubricating a valve stem, cleaning a strainer, replacing a damaged sprinkler.

Where things live in the standard (2023 edition): Chapter 4 — general requirements (responsibilities, impairments, records); Chapter 5 — sprinkler systems; Chapter 6 — standpipe and hose systems; Chapter 7 — private fire service mains; Chapter 8 — fire pumps; Chapter 9 — water storage tanks; Chapters 10–12 — water spray, foam-water, and water mist; Chapter 13 — valves, valve components, and trim. Later chapters cover internal piping inspection, obstruction investigation, and impairments; numbering past Chapter 13 varies by edition, so check the table of contents in your copy.

Level I rule of thumb: you identify, inspect, record, and operate under direct supervision. You report what you find — you do not decide what it means or change how the system is set.
Inspection and test frequencies every Level I technician should know

Frequencies are a large part of the exam. Learn them by interval, then use the ITM frequencies tab to drill them. These are the NFPA 25 (2023) intervals most relevant to a Level I technician.

IntervalWhat happens
WeeklyInspect sealed control valves. Inspect gauges on dry, preaction, and deluge systems.
MonthlyInspect locked and tamper-switch-supervised control valves. Inspect wet-system gauges.
QuarterlyTest mechanical waterflow alarms (water motor gong). Inspect the fire department connection (FDC).
SemiannuallyTest vane-type and pressure-switch waterflow alarms. Test valve supervisory (tamper) switches.
AnnuallyOperate control valves through their full range. Main drain test. Visual inspection of sprinklers, pipe, hangers, bracing, signs, and spare sprinklers. Inspect and flush hydrants.
Every 5 yearsReplace or calibrate gauges.
Memory aid: sealed = weekly; locked or tamper-switch = monthly. The more protection a valve has against being closed unnoticed, the less often it must be looked at.

Heated valve enclosures must hold at least 40°F (4°C); in freezing weather they are checked daily, or weekly where a monitored low-temperature alarm is provided. Intervals can be shortened by the AHJ or the manufacturer, and some editions word exceptions differently — confirm in the adopted edition.

Control valves in depth

A closed control valve is one of the most common reasons a sprinkler system fails to control a fire. Valve checks are the most frequent ITM task for a reason.

ValveHow you tell it is openNotes
OS&Y (outside screw and yoke)Stem projects out through the yoke.Stem withdrawn into the body = shut.
PIV / WPIV (post or wall post indicator)Target window reads OPEN.Operated with a key/wrench at the post; used on underground supply.
Butterfly valveGear-operator indicator or position flag shows open.Almost always has a supervisory switch; do not rely on handle position alone if the indicator disagrees.
Ball or quarter-turn valveHandle in line with the pipe.Common on drains, test connections, and small trim.
  • Sealed valves have a breakable seal and are checked weekly. Locked valves (chain and lock) and electrically supervised valves (tamper switch) are checked monthly.
  • Exercise each control valve through its full range once a year, return it to normal, and confirm the supervisory signal clears. Backing the handwheel off a quarter turn after opening prevents jamming on some valves.
  • Tamper switches are tested semiannually: the signal should start within two turns of the handwheel or one-fifth of the valve travel from normal.
  • Check every valve for an identification sign, accessibility, leaks, and damage.
If you find a control valve closed, partly closed, or not where the records say it should be, stop and tell your supervising technician. Do not reopen it on your own — the system may have been intentionally impaired, and reopening a valve on a charged riser has to be controlled.
Alarm, supervisory, and trouble signals — and how alarms are tested
SignalMeaningTypical causes
AlarmWater is flowing in the system.Sprinkler operation, inspector’s test, drain flow.
SupervisoryA monitored condition is off-normal.Closed or tampered valve, low air pressure, low tank level or temperature.
TroubleA fault in the monitoring circuit or equipment.Open circuit, ground fault, device removed, power or battery problem.
  • Mechanical alarm (water motor gong) — tested quarterly. Water through the alarm line spins the motor and strikes the gong. Check that the line, strainer, and drain are clear and nothing leaks.
  • Vane-type and pressure-switch alarms — tested semiannually. Confirm the signal reaches the panel and the monitoring company.
  • Inspector’s test connection (ITC) — a test outlet with an orifice sized to match the smallest sprinkler on the system, so flowing it simulates one sprinkler operating. It is usually at the hydraulically remote end of the system.
  • Always tell the monitoring company before you test, and confirm afterward that signals have cleared and the panel is back to normal.
Sprinklers, pipe, hangers, and signs — what a good visual inspection finds

Sprinklers and piping are inspected annually from the floor. Look at every sprinkler you can see and note anything that could keep it from operating or from spraying correctly.

  • Deficiencies to flag: corrosion, paint or coatings, dust/grease/lint loading, leaks, bent or missing deflectors, damaged or loose escutcheons, glass bulbs with lost liquid, wrong orientation (pendent versus upright), stored items or building changes blocking the spray, and sprinklers of mixed or incorrect types in the same area.
  • Sprinkler guards and shields are inspected annually too (a requirement added in the 2023 edition).
  • Pipe and fittings: leaks, corrosion, mechanical damage, loose or missing hangers, misalignment, and loads hung from pipe.
  • Signs: hydraulic nameplate, general information sign, and valve identification signs must be present, legible, and attached.
  • Spare sprinklers: cabinet with the correct types and temperature ratings, plus the right wrench, kept no warmer than 100°F (38°C). Typical spare quantities: fewer than 300 sprinklers → 6; 300–1,000 → 12; over 1,000 → 24.

The main drain test and hydrant flushing — steps and what you record

Main drain test (annually, and quarterly downstream of a backflow preventer or PRV where it is the only supply):

  1. Notify the owner and monitoring company; confirm the drain discharge is directed to a safe location.
  2. Record the supply (static) pressure with the main control valve fully open and the drain closed.
  3. Open the main drain fully and let it flow until the pressure stabilizes; record the residual pressure.
  4. Close the drain slowly to avoid water hammer; confirm pressure recovers to static in a reasonable time.
  5. Compare static and residual to the previous test. A noticeably larger drop can mean a partly closed valve or a developing obstruction — report it.
  6. Return everything to normal and clear the notifications.

Hydrant flush (annually): open the hydrant fully, flow until the water runs clear, close slowly, and confirm a dry-barrel hydrant drains. Inspect caps, gaskets, threads, and operating nut, and report leaks or damage.

Safety and impairments at Level I — before, during, and after
  • Before: get your assignment and the system information from your supervising technician; confirm who is notifying the owner, monitoring company, and (where required) the fire department; review hazards (water discharge, ladders and lifts, electrical, confined spaces); wear the required PPE.
  • During: if a task will close a valve or take any part of the system out of service, it is an impairment — the supervising technician coordinates tagging and notification. Keep a clear exit path and keep water away from electrical equipment.
  • After: return every valve to normal, confirm gauges and supervisory signals are back to normal, remove tags, and tell the people who were notified that the system is restored.

When a system is out of service for more than 10 hours in a 24-hour period, additional emergency measures apply (for example, a fire watch). This is managed at a higher level, but you should know why impairments are tracked and ended promptly.

Preliminary documentation — what to capture and common mistakes
  • Identify the correct report for the task, then record: building and system identification; riser location; valve type, size, make, and model; sprinkler type, K-factor, temperature rating, and quantity of each; alarm and supervisory device types; and gauge readings with units.
  • Record observations, not conclusions: “OS&Y valve stem retracted, valve appears shut” rather than “valve broken.”
  • Note anything missing (signs, caps, spare sprinkler wrench) and anything that looks off even if you are unsure.

Common mistakes: recording a gauge without units, copying last year’s values, skipping “normal” readings, leaving a valve in the wrong position, and not photographing a deficiency.

Exam strategy for Level I
  • Expect component-identification and “what do you do next?” questions. The correct answer almost always involves reporting to a supervising technician and notifying before operating.
  • Eliminate answers that have a Level I technician approving, adjusting, or deciding something that belongs to a higher level.
  • Memorize the interval ladder: weekly → monthly → quarterly → semiannual → annual → 5 years, and which items sit on each rung.
  • Watch for look-alikes: sealed versus locked, mechanical versus electric alarms, static versus residual.

How to interpret this comparison

Drop = static − residual. A larger drop than the previous main drain test can indicate a supply-side restriction, such as a partially closed valve or a developing obstruction. A number alone does not establish a cause.

Before concluding anything, confirm the control valves are fully open, the test was taken at the same point under full flow, and the gauge is serviceable — then report the finding to your supervising technician.
