Study guides

Code-use reminder: This guide intentionally summarizes concepts rather than reproducing NFPA text. Exact intervals, acceptance criteria, exceptions, and section numbers must be checked in your licensed NFPA 25 (2023) copy.
2.1 — Safety

Be ready to explain: how a Level II technician implements (not just recognizes) safety procedures while performing ITM with limited supervision.

  • Implement proper use and maintenance of PPE and lockout/tagout (LOTO).
  • Address work hazards and apply procedures for confined spaces, openings, lighting, ceilings, ladders, and lifts.
  • Verify correct tools and equipment are used and maintained, and ensure safe ladder use.
  • Implement safety and system impairment procedures before, during, and after testing.
2.2 — Communication

At Level II you coordinate the people and parties affected by an inspection, not just the equipment.

  • Coordinate with customers — pre-inspection reviews, scope of work, and notifications.
  • Verify that the alarm company, owner, and AHJ are contacted for testing and maintenance that affects monitoring or takes the system out of service.
  • Document and communicate the results and findings of ITM tasks to the customer or responsible party.
Notification is a recurring exam theme: before any test that trips a signal or impairs a system, confirm the monitoring company and owner are informed, and confirm restoration afterward.
2.3 — Perform, document, and interpret intermediate ITM tasks

This is the largest Level II domain and covers routine ITM across system types, plus classifying what you find.

  • Dry systems: inspect and test quick-opening devices (QODs), air compressors, nitrogen generators, and air maintenance devices; understand the purpose of a trip test and how air pressure and priming water affect it.
  • Wet systems: perform ITM including antifreeze systems and automatic air vents; know how antifreeze solution is sampled and checked.
  • Standpipe systems: perform ITM and recognize the class of standpipe (occupant use, fire-department use, or both).
  • Deficiencies: identify and classify findings as critical, non-critical, or impairment, and apply the correct handling and notification for each.
2.4 — Perform, document, and interpret ITM tasks for water supplies

Water-supply ITM at Level II covers the source and delivery path: pumps, mains, and tanks.

  • Fire pumps: perform routine inspection and testing; understand the no-flow (churn), rated (100%), and overload (150%) points and what each one checks.
  • Private fire service mains: perform ITM including flow testing and forward-flow verification of backflow preventers where applicable.
  • Water storage tanks: perform ITM of tank components, levels, and related equipment.
  • Record readings against the correct baseline and the applicable acceptance criteria, and account for differences in test conditions.
2.5 — Internal obstruction investigation

At Level II you determine when an investigation is warranted and assess the internal condition of piping — a foundation for the more complex analysis expected at Level III.

  • Determine the need for an obstruction investigation and recognize conditions that support prevention.
  • Plan safe access and impairment handling before opening piping.
  • Assess and document the internal condition of piping — deposits, corrosion products, tuberculation, or foreign material — without overdiagnosing the cause.
Opening piping can impair protection and release stored pressure or water. Follow the approved impairment and safety process, and restore the system afterward.

Wet systems — ITM in depth
  • Gauges: inspected monthly for good condition and normal supply pressure; replaced or calibrated every 5 years (replace if off by more than 3% of full scale).
  • Waterflow alarms: mechanical alarms quarterly; vane and pressure-switch alarms semiannually. Flow the inspector’s test connection and confirm both local and remote signals. Trace a slow or silent gong to a plugged alarm line, strainer, or orifice.
  • Main drain test: annually at each riser and after any control valve is closed and reopened. Compare static and residual to the previous test and investigate a meaningful change in drop. Test quarterly on at least one riser downstream of a backflow preventer or PRV that is the only supply.
  • Antifreeze systems: test the solution annually before freezing weather by checking the freeze point at the top and bottom of each loop. New and refilled loops use listed premixed solutions — do not mix glycerin or glycol on site.
  • Automatic air vents and trapped air: repeated false flow-switch signals with no real flow often trace to trapped air; confirm vents function.
  • Five-year items: alarm and check valve interiors, strainers and orifices, gauges, and the internal piping assessment.
  • Sample testing: follow the age-based schedule for sprinklers (standard-response 50 years, fast-response 25 years in the 2023 edition, then every 10). If any sprinkler in a representative sample fails, all sprinklers in the area that sample represents must be replaced.
Always return the inspector’s test valve, drains, and control valves to normal after testing, and verify that signals have cleared before you leave.

  • Record at every trip test: system air pressure before the test, air pressure at which the valve tripped, water supply pressure, time to trip, and (full flow) time for water to reach the test connection; compare to the previous test.
  • Quick-opening devices speed the loss of system air so the valve trips sooner. After testing, reset the device and return all isolation and trim valves to normal — an isolated QOD is a hidden impairment.
  • Priming water seals the clapper against air leakage; restore the correct level after every trip.
  • Slow or late trip — investigate: air pressure higher than the manufacturer’s chart for the supply pressure, QOD isolated or malfunctioning, partly closed test valve or control valve, incorrect priming water, or leaks and condensation affecting the system.
  • Nitrogen and compressors: confirm the supply keeps the required supervisory air pressure without short cycling; frequent compressor starts point to air leaks.
Standpipe systems — classes, types, and ITM

Classes: Class I — 2½″ hose connections for fire department use. Class II — 1½″ connections for occupants or trained personnel. Class III — both.

Types: automatic wet, automatic dry, semiautomatic dry, manual wet, and manual dry. The type tells you how water reaches the hose connections and which tests apply.

A standpipe flow test only means something when documented against the design requirement: record the outlet flow, residual pressure at the test outlet, the static pressure, and the location of the test.
Fire pumps — routine inspection and the three test points
  • Weekly pump house visual: room heat and ventilation, suction and discharge valves open, gauges normal, controller power light on and no alarms. Diesel: fuel level, oil, coolant, batteries, and charger.
  • No-flow (churn) run: electric pumps monthly for about 10 minutes; diesel pumps weekly for about 30 minutes. Confirm automatic start from the pressure drop, record suction and discharge pressure, amperage (electric) or engine readings (diesel), and watch packing, bearings, and unusual noise or vibration.
  • Annual flow test: run the pump at three points — churn (0%), rated (100%), and peak (150%) flow — recording pressure, flow, speed, and driver data at each.
  • What the numbers should look like: churn pressure is typically no more than about 140% of rated pressure, and at 150% of rated flow the pump should still deliver at least about 65% of rated pressure. Compare results to the pump’s unadjusted field acceptance curve (after correcting for speed); a decline of more than 5% needs investigation.
  • Jockey pump maintains system pressure and starts first; the fire pump starts if pressure keeps falling. Frequent jockey cycling points to system leaks.
Never operate or defeat a fire pump controller, isolating switch, or transfer switch without understanding the lockout/tagout and notification steps. A pump left in the wrong mode after testing is an impairment.
Private fire service mains, hydrants, and water storage tanks

Private mains and hydrants

  • Hydrants: inspect annually and after each operation; flow and flush annually until the water runs clear; confirm dry-barrel drainage; lubricate and exercise per the manufacturer.
  • Mainline strainers: clean annually and after each operation.
  • Underground piping: flow test every 5 years at the rate expected in a fire, compare to the previous test, and investigate a decline. Control valves (PIVs, underground, and curb-box valves) follow the same weekly/monthly inspection and annual exercise as other control valves.
  • After new work or repairs, flush before connecting to avoid carrying debris into the system.

Deficiencies, impairments, and communication
ClassMeaningExamplesHandling
ImpairmentAll or part of the system is out of order and may not work in a fire.Closed riser valve; shut-off pump; isolated QOD.Impairment procedure, tag, notify, restore as fast as possible.
Critical deficiencyIf not corrected, will adversely affect or cause failure of the system.Pump will not start; corroded or obstructed piping; failed alarm path.Immediate notice to the owner; follow-up promptly.
Non-critical deficiencyDoes not meet the impairment or critical definition but reduces performance or compliance.Missing sign; damaged FDC cap; minor leak.Report in writing and schedule correction.
  • Before impairing: use the impairment coordinator process, tag the valve, and notify the owner, monitoring company, and fire department (and the insurer where required).
  • If the impairment exceeds 10 hours in a 24-hour period, emergency measures (such as a fire watch or temporary protection) apply until the system is restored.
  • Records: document what was done, results, deficiencies, and corrective actions, and keep them at least 1 year after the next like inspection, test, or maintenance.
  • Plain-language reporting: state the finding, where it is, what it affects, and what you recommend. Separate facts from opinion.
Internal piping assessment and obstruction investigation
  • Five-year internal assessment: open a flushing connection at the end of a main and several branch lines (wet systems) and examine the interior for deposits, scale, corrosion, and foreign material.
  • Triggers for an obstruction investigation include: obstructive material discharged during drain tests or flushing; plugged sprinklers or inspector’s test openings; foreign material in a dry pipe valve, check valve, or pump; pinhole leaks that suggest microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC); failure to flush new or repaired mains; and return to service after an extended shutdown.
  • Plan access, impairment, drainage, and restoration before opening piping, and document representative findings with photos and locations.
  • Report conditions without over-diagnosing the cause; the cause analysis is a Level III task.
Exam strategy for Level II
  • Level II questions combine a task, an interval, and a reaction to a result. If a result is off, the best answer is to verify the setup and conditions first, compare to the previous result, then report — not to adjust equipment.
  • Know the difference between inspection intervals and test intervals for the same device (for example, dry system gauges are inspected weekly while the dry valve is trip-tested annually).
  • Expect to place events on the right rung: quarterly (gong, FDC, priming water, low-air, QOD) versus semiannual (vane/pressure switch, tamper switch).
  • Look for notification: before anything that trips a signal or impairs protection, the owner and monitoring company must know.

How to interpret this comparison

Compare net pressure at churn (no flow), rated (100%), and overload (150%) against the pump's rated/baseline values. A meaningful shortfall at a flow point warrants evaluation, but confirm the flow rate, gauge accuracy, suction conditions, and test setup first.

A single reading does not establish compliance or a cause. Record conditions and compare like-for-like against the correct baseline and acceptance criteria.
